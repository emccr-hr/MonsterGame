# Genre Research: what makes monster-catching games fun

*Researched 2026-11-21 to guide suggestions. **The designer decides the game.** This page is for
ideas and lessons only. **Nothing here is copied into the game** (Flags N1).* Sources are at the bottom.

## The game he named as inspiration (2018, Nintendo Switch)
A simplified, newcomer-friendly take on its series:
- **Wild monsters are visible on the map.** No random encounters: you walk up to the one you want.
- **Catching is a throwing mini-game**, about timing rather than aim. It's hard to fail, and catches give XP.
- **Catch combos:** catching the same species in a row raises the odds of rare and special versions.
- **Partner creature** rides with you and can be dressed up. Others can follow you around.
- **Simpler systems:** breeding and similar extras removed.
- **Co-op:** a second player can join with a second controller.
- Critics liked the charm, the pacing and the easy start. They disliked that it was **too easy**, that catching got **repetitive**, and the **required motion controls**.

## Why the genre is fun
1. **Collecting.** A visible collection (a monster book) that fills up is a strong pull.
2. **Rarity and surprise.** "Something rare might be next" keeps people playing. *(Ours: Big Monsters, 1 in 100.)*
3. **Attachment.** A favorite monster you raise, name, and see on screen.
4. **Growth payoff.** Evolution is a visible reward you earned. *(Ours: per-monster evolve levels.)*
5. **Battle decisions.** Most games use strong/weak types. *(Ours: **no types**, which is the designer's choice. Moves, abilities, Burn and Freeze, Fetch, Dig and switching give the choices instead.)*
6. **Exploring different places**, each with its own monsters. *(Ours: 5 biomes.)*
7. **Short loops:** spot, catch, grow, win, all in a few minutes.

## Designing for ages 8–12
- **Little reading:** short lines, icons, skippable text.
- **Always show the next goal**, such as an arrow or a "next" hint. Kids get frustrated by not knowing *where to go*, not by the challenge itself.
- **No punishment for failing.** Lose a battle, heal at the Reviver, lose nothing. *(Ours already does this.)*
- **Teach by doing**, one thing at a time.
- **Encourage often.** Young players blame themselves when stuck.
- **10–20 minute sessions.** Save often (autosave).
- **No twitch-reflex or button-mashing requirements.** Keyboard and mouse should both work, and nothing should rely on color alone.
- **Playtest with kids early.** Designers almost always make games too hard at first.

## Pitfalls for small monster games
| Pitfall | How we avoid it |
|---|---|
| Scope creep | Design is frozen for v1; new ideas go to the Ideas Parking Lot |
| Too many monsters | 30 is already a lot; art plan is base forms first, placeholders for the rest |
| Grind | Shared team XP, level scaling (X18), no level walls |
| Slow battles | **Speed-up toggle** and quick text from day one |
| Too easy | Rare Big Monsters, Avatar as a real challenge, optional tough trainers (idea) |

## Ideas from this research for the designer to consider
These are suggestions only. Each is listed in `09-Production/Open-Questions.md` section F for him to pick.
1. **See wild monsters walking around** instead of surprise encounters.
2. **Catch streaks:** catching the same monster several times in a row raises your Big Monster chance a little.
3. **Partner monster** follows you around on the map.
4. **Cage-throw timing:** a shrinking circle where a well-timed throw gets a catch bonus. It's never required.
5. **Monster Book** showing which monsters you've seen and caught (helps with the gate to Avatar).
6. **Battle speed-up** button.
7. **Guide character** in Ansonville who gives tips and says what to do next.
8. **Optional strong trainers** in each biome, for players who want a challenge.
